The Problem
On a Mars mission, Earth is too far to be a first responder. A signal takes up to 22 minutes to arrive, so a call for help and a reply can take 44 minutes or more. For a cardiac event, a radiation-driven crisis or a sudden neurological problem, that gap can decide the outcome.
Moreover, astronauts are expected to run a mission and also act as their own doctors. In a cramped habitat, with altered gravity, fatigue and a heavy workload, they cannot reliably or regularly assess their own condition, even when diagnostic equipment is on board.
So the real need is not more medical equipment. It is continuous, automatic, local health awareness that asks nothing of the crew.
Our Solution
We built a two-part system:
Hardware: a tri-node wearable network (head, chest, wrist) that measures continuously and is always ready on the body.
Software (RCPT): an offline web application that fuses those signals with radiation and sleep data, scores crew risk in real time, and explains every number.
Everything runs locally on the ship. Nothing depends on the signal to Earth.
How the System Works
Step 1. Sense. Head and chest nodes capture microvolt-level brain (EEG) and heart (ECG) signals and clean them locally.
Step 2. Aggregate. They send compact data packets over Bluetooth Low Energy to the wrist hub, which adds blood pressure, oxygen saturation and in-suit environment readings.
Step 3. Transmit. The wrist hub sends one synchronized telemetry packet through the suit by RF link to the habitatâ€™s local server.
Step 4. Understand. RCPT software combines the telemetry with radiation dose and sleep debt and computes a live risk index from 0 to 100.
Step 5. Act. The crew sees the score, the affected organ systems and a plain-language explanation, by screen or by voice, and gets the right alert tier: nominal, elevated or emergency.

Why this design works
Edge processing first. Head and chest nodes filter and digitize their own signals, so only clean, compact data travels. That saves power and bandwidth.
One hub, one link. Only the wrist hub talks to the habitat, which keeps the wireless footprint small and the data synchronized.
Always ready. The nodes are worn continuously, so the system already has a baseline when an emergency begins.
Built for the Martian environment
Radiation hardening: silicon-on-insulator microcontrollers and error-correcting memory protect against bit-flips from cosmic rays.
Thermal control: electronics are encapsulated in thermally conductive, electrically insulating epoxy that passes heat into the suitâ€™s liquid cooling garment.
Dust protection: sealed housings (IP69K-equivalent) with PTFE membranes keep out abrasive, electrostatically charged regolith.
Low-gravity securement: elastomeric tensioners and micro-boa dials hold electrodes against the skin. Capacitive sensors track skin contact and warn the wearer if a node loses it.

What Is Real and What Is Simulated
In our demo, we donâ€™t have actual hardware, so sensor inputs are simulated from three parameters: sleep debt, radiation exposure and mission time.
In a flight version, the same inputs would come live from the tri-node hardware and crew dosimetry.
Outputs are decision support, not diagnosis. RCPT flags risk and explains it. It does not replace medical judgment.
Conclusion
The difference our solution makes is that health awareness moves from Earth to the crew itself. Instead of waiting 44 minutes for an answer, astronauts get a continuous, explainable picture of their own condition, built from light-weight wearable sensors and software that never needs a signal home. It is simple enough to use under stress, and stand by to act in emergencies.
